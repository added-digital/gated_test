# Thaw

*Angle: start from the constraint.* You keep a hot-spring tea house at the foot of a glacier that is slowly giving back everything anyone ever lost on the mountain, and you walk into the ice with a pot of coals to bring those things home to the people still waiting for them.

**The player carries a pot of coals into the glacier to bring lost things home to the people waiting for them before the coals die.**

## The surface

The home is Ume-ya, a two-room tea house with a steaming outdoor bath at the foot of the glacier, in the last weeks of winter: snow on the eaves, the two sakura bare, Pim asleep on the hearth stone. By day the pace is slow and warm: you set the pot back in the hearth, lay out what you carried on the Shelf, feed scraps to the fire, and talk to whoever is on the engawa (Bunta smoking a pipe he never lights, Kana swearing at a broken instrument, a traveler who came up the road because they heard this is where things come back). At dusk the pace flips: travelers arrive in a small rush, each with one line about what they lost, and you match descriptions to objects on the Shelf while the lanterns burn down (the Help toy's clock and the Remember toy's quoted lines, finally with a reason). A right match is a return: a short scene, a reward, and Pim writes a line in the Register. What grows is visible: the Register fills, the sakura go from bare to bud to bloom, the road down the mountain reopens, the second room fills with overnight guests, and the things nobody claimed spill out of the house into the stone garden as a museum of lost objects. Who is there: Bunta, Pim, Kana, later Tetsu, and every claimant is a named person who remembers what you gave them.

## The underground

Behind the back gate the hot spring's steam stops at a line of frost and the mouth of the glacier begins: Shirakura, the White Storehouse. You walk into blue ice carrying the ember pot; its glow is both your light and your warmth, and there is no bar on screen: you read the glow, the length of your breath, and the frost creeping in at the screen's edges. The pace is slow and careful: crunch, drip, the tick of moving ice, and somewhere far off a lost thing making a sound (a cracked bell, a music box, a radio hiss) that you hear long before you see it, which is the Far Sound toy with a reward at the end. Things are frozen into the walls as dark silhouettes; you chip them out with a pick while the coals burn, choosing between windy gaps that drain the pot fast and dripping tunnels that are long but sheltered, and warming up at the hot vents that pierce the ice. You gather lost things from every century: sandals, bells, letters, cameras, a helmet, a bicycle, a mail bag, bones, and sometimes the sled you lost yourself three trips ago. Each visit the ice has moved: plugs have refrozen some corridors and opened others, a vent has migrated, a pool has formed where a floor was, a new silhouette shows in a wall that was blank. The deeper tiers (the White, the Blue, the Black near the vents, the Old Ice) are colder each step and need a better coat.

## The threshold

A stone path through the garden, over the 3 m bridge, to a small back gate. On the home side the stones are wet and dark from the spring's steam; on the far side they are rimed white, and your breath appears the moment you cross. You can only cross while carrying the pot, and the pot lives in the hearth, so leaving is a physical act: you pick the coals up out of your own fire, walk through the steam, and step over the frost line. The sound changes from the pond's trickle to the ice's tick. Coming back you cross into steam, the frost vignette thaws off the screen, your breath vanishes, and you set the pot down in the hearth; that set-down is the return, every single time.

## Three dials

- COALS (how long): the ember pot's heat. Glow radius equals warmth equals how long you can stay; it drains at base rate, twice as fast in wind gaps, three times as fast wading meltwater, and pauses at a hot vent. You can spend it (hold to blow: double light radius for 3 seconds, costs 10 seconds of coals) or feed it (drop any wooden or cloth find into the pot for +25%, the find is gone). As it falls, your body degrades in stages: breath thickens, you hunch, hands shake so chipping takes twice as long, then hands cannot grip at all, then legs slow. Upgrade track: clay pot, iron pot with a wind lid, binchotan charcoal that burns slow, Bunta's old brazier.
- COAT (how far): cold tiers of the glacier. The White is survivable in hemp; the Blue freezes your hands in seconds without padding; the Black near the vents is wet and needs oilskin; the Old Ice needs fur. Each coat opens one tier and the objects frozen in it, and the coat is visible on your back. Upgrade track: hemp jacket, padded coat, oilskin lining, bear fur. The pick (what you can take: ice pick, axe, saw) is a fourth track outside the dials, delivered by Kana.
- SLED (how much): what you can drag out. Weight slows you and raises the coals' drain per metre, so a full sled across a windy gap is a bet. Upgrade track: hands (1 item), back basket (3), sled (6, rides down ice slopes), runnered sled (8, faster on flats, worse in wind).

## The fail state is a bet

When the coals die the cold takes you: the screen whites out, and you wake on the tatami with the pot cold beside you and your boots mysteriously dry; nobody says who carried you (that is a later character). You keep exactly one thing: whatever you tucked into the coat's breast pocket, a deliberate one-button act you perform in the ice while your hands still work. The sled and everything on it stays in the glacier, and the glacier keeps everything, so your own lost sled can turn up frozen in a wall trips later with your stuff still on it. The bet is made twice on every hard trip: whether to burn a find to buy coals for the walk home, and which single thing goes in the pocket when you push for the silhouette past the blue wall.

## Conversion and two sinks

Everything you carry out goes on the Shelf. Sink one is the Shelf itself: things with owners are returned at dusk through claims (a reward, a Register line, a named person who now remembers you), and things without owners are sold to Bunta, later Tetsu, for the money that buys pots, coats, sleds and picks. Sink two is the Hearth: anything organic (wood, cloth, paper, bone, the tenth sandal) feeds the fire and banks coals, so your next trip starts hotter and longer; over-collecting never wastes. The two sinks squeeze each other on purpose: a claimable thing can be burned for coals or sold to Tetsu for triple, and Pim notices ("That was somebody's.").

## The second progression

The Register: the tea house's lost-and-found book that Pim keeps, one line per returned thing, written in the owner's words. It is not money and cannot be bought. Its count decides what the home becomes: the two bare sakura bud at 5 returns, half-bloom at 15, full bloom at 40; the road down the mountain reopens at 10 (more claimants arrive, Tetsu drives up); travelers can stay the night at 20 (the second room opens, and overnight claims come from people who are dead); the outdoor bath is rebuilt at 30; the spring festival at 40 ends chapter one. Spring arrives at Ume-ya exactly as fast as things go home.

## What breaks the calm

Three scales. Every trip: lifting the coals out of your own hearth and stepping over the frost line is the break, the dimming glow is the tension, the cold is the thing that will end it. Every day: dusk, when travelers arrive wanting something back and the lanterns start burning down. Every chapter: the glacier groans and calves; the hot spring's vein is blocked, the bath goes cold, snow blows onto the tatami, and the only fix is inside the ice. The chapter-one climax is exactly this, scheduled at a Register count, not left to the player's autonomy.

## Where accomplishment lands

Twice on every loop. In the body: the moment you re-cross the frost line into steam, the frost thaws off the screen edges, your breath disappears, and you set the pot back into the hearth. Then socially: at dusk a named person holds the thing you carried out, says one line about it, Pim writes it in the Register, and a bud opens on a sakura. The second landing is the fix for Hwang's regret: no customer is nameless, every claim is a person who remembers what you brought them and quotes it back weeks later.

## The one-map conceit

Shirakura, the White Storehouse: a glacier that has flowed past this spot for ten thousand years and kept everything that ever fell into it, from a Jomon pot to a 1962 expedition camera to a mail bag dropped in 1986 to the sled you lost last week, and the hot spring underneath is melting it out from below, one chamber a season. Any object from any era, with any owner living, dead or descended, can be added forever as one entry: a silhouette in a wall, a sound, a description, three to six lines of dialogue, a spot in the ice. No new system is ever needed. New areas are new seasons of melt in the same glacier, and the shifting ice is just plugs toggled per visit from a seed.

## Characters and requests

Bunta (70s, ex-mountain postman, your great-uncle, owner of Ume-ya, pipe never lit): wants the last letter from his wife, dropped with his post bag into a crevasse in 1986. Introduces the loop itself: the ember pot, the one rule, the breast pocket, the Hearth, and buying unclaimed things ("I send them down the mountain"). Pim (the small hearth-stone creature that remembers every word you say, the Remember toy with a job): wants the sakura to bloom again ("they did once, when the book was full"). Introduces the Register and dusk claims, later the overnight claims from the dead. Kana (28, glaciologist camped in the stone garden with a broken instrument and a loud mouth): wants her grandmother's camera from the 1962 expedition. Introduces the Coat and the cold tiers, then the axe, then the sled and the map of the ice. Tetsu (antiques dealer who drives up the day the road reopens; buys anything, pays triple for things that have owners): introduces the shop proper, the Wants board, and the moral squeeze between the two sinks. Every later system arrives as one of these four asking for something, with a scene, at a scheduled Register count; nothing is a menu unlock.

## Two lines

- Bunta: "Third sandal this week. Either somebody up there had a lot of feet, or you've got a type."
- Bunta: "You said 'quick trip.' I lit the lanterns. I put them out. I lit them again. Quick."

## Later, in order

- The Blue (Kana asks for her grandmother's camera): the padded coat opens cold tier two, the ice pick becomes an axe, first deep silhouettes and the first object that needs two trips.
- The road reopens (Register 10): Tetsu arrives with the shop and the Wants board; money becomes a real sink; the Shelf spills into the stone garden as a museum of unclaimed things.
- The sled (Kana): the how-much dial, ice slopes you can ride down, a crevasse chamber, and your own lost sled starting to appear in walls.
- Overnight claims (Pim, Register 20): travelers can stay the night; the dead come to claim at the hour the lanterns are out; the memory flags get a second column.
- The Carrier (Bunta: 'find out who brings you back'): the character who carries you home is revealed; fire-making in the ice, a camp built from burnable finds that becomes a checkpoint you made yourself.
- The Black and the cold bath (chapter-one climax, scheduled): the glacier calves, the spring's vein is blocked, the bath goes cold; the oilskin opens the wet deep by the vents; clearing the vein restores the home's warmth.
- Seasons of melt: after each chapter the glacier rearranges and exposes an era cache (the pilgrim century, the war year, the 1980s); new objects and owners, zero new systems.
- The Old Ice and full bloom (Register 40): bear fur, the oldest chamber, Bunta's letter, the spring festival; end of chapter one and the natural early-access ship point.

## Presentation

Low-poly 3D in Godot 4.6, fixed three-quarter camera that rotates in 90-degree steps (A Short Hike's view, not Dave's side-on 2D). Why: the kit already exists as low-poly glTF, so the home is free; the whole constraint is one OmniLight on the pot plus volumetric fog and a frost vignette shader, which Godot gives cheaply and 2D cannot (light radius as the gauge, a vertical glacier, ice slopes to sled down, breath as a particle); a fixed camera removes camera engineering and keeps every chamber readable; GDScript is something Claude writes reliably and Godot is free and ships to PC and Switch-class hardware. The engine question is settled: Godot, yes.

## The kit

Adapted, not dropped. The 27-piece tea house is Ume-ya as-is: tatami, shoji, engawa, pond, bridge, stone garden, bamboo, lanterns, props. Three changes: (1) the two sakura trees get four states (bare, bud, half, full) because they are the Register's meter; (2) a snow pass over the kit (snow caps for roof eaves, stone lanterns, bridge rails, about 8 overlay pieces) because the home starts in late winter and thaws; (3) a hearth, a steaming bath piece and a back gate. Cost: about 3 Claude days plus validator passes, with the style contract extended by a snow palette. The glacier needs a new kit of about 18 pieces (ice wall modules, floor, plug, crevasse edge, ice bridge, vent, drip pool, stalactite, drift, frozen-object shells): about 5 Claude days with the contract extended by an ice palette. None of this is needed for the grey-box loop toy, which must prove the loop before any of it is made.

## The loop toy

Rooms: 1 home (a grey plane with a hearth marker, a 6-slot Shelf, an engawa edge, a back gate) and 1 glacier of 5 chambers (mouth, windy gap, dripping tunnel, bell chamber, blue wall with one silhouette beyond it), joined so there are two routes to the bell chamber. Items: 8 found things frozen in walls as grey shapes, each with a name, a one-line description and a sound (sandal, prayer plaque, bow: burnable; comb, bell, teacup, helmet, camera: not), plus 1 ember pot, 1 hemp coat, 1 basket (3 slots), 1 pick. Characters: 3 (Bunta: gives the pot, the rule, buys unclaimed things; Pim: keeps the Register and has 6 memory lines keyed to what you said; Hana: the single dusk claim, the bell). Upgrades: 2, both reachable inside 20 minutes (iron pot: +50% coals; padded coat: opens the blue wall). Systems: ember glow as light radius and warmth with wind at double drain; blow; feed; pocket; white-out and wake with the pocketed item; chip-out on a hold; one dusk claim with 3 candidate objects; a Register counter with a grey sakura sphere that turns pink. Claude build time: 6 days (days 1-2 movement, pot, glow, wind, blow, feed, pocket, white-out; day 3 chambers, plugs that toggle per visit, silhouettes, chipping; day 4 home: hearth, Shelf, Bunta's buying, 2 upgrades; day 5 Pim, Register, Hana's claim, memory lines; day 6 tuning). The single question a 20-minute play answers: with no bar on screen, does the pot's glow alone make the player turn back by themselves, and then go straight back in for the thing they saw and left?

## Hypothesis line

I believe carrying a dimming pot of coals into grey ice, chipping out one thing, walking back to a hearth, handing it to a named person, buying a bigger pot and going back for what you left, with grey boxes, holds a confidant 20 minutes because the pot's glow makes every step out a visible bet with no bar on screen and the thing you saw past the blue wall is a promise the home can cash; dead if not by 2026-11-06.

## Shared with Dave, and yours

Structurally shared, on purpose: a calm surface and a risky out separated by a threshold you cross deliberately; three dials on one trip, each with its own upgrade track; a fail state that keeps one chosen item; two sinks so over-collecting never wastes; a non-money reputation that decides what the home becomes; a one-map conceit that absorbs content forever; every system as a request from a named character with a scene; side activities scheduled at story moments; opposite paces for the two halves. Yours: the constraint is a glowing object you can see and handle rather than a bar, with three physical verbs on it (blow, feed, pocket) and a drain driven by terrain, so route choice is the game; the haul has owners, so conversion is social deduction and memory, not cooking; the home grows as literal spring, bare to bloom; the out is a river of ice that moves, with the home's own warmth at stake when it calves; the thing you lose comes back; the voice is Undertale-cocky; no water, no fish, no boat, no restaurant.

## Three ways it fails

- The ember reads as a timer in a nicer costume: if wind, meltwater and vents do not change the drain enough to make routes matter, players watch the glow like a bar and the constraint is no longer the most interesting thing in the game. The toy exists to catch this; if it fails the drain modifiers are doubled before anything else is touched.
- Claims are too thin as the home half, the Moonlighter failure: if descriptions are plain lookups the dusk scene becomes a chore, and if they are too puzzly they block the loop. The fix is writing (misdirection, humour, memory) and a hard cap of one tricky claim per evening; dead if playtesters start skipping dusk.
- Content cost lands on writing, not systems: every lost thing is an object plus an owner plus three to six lines in a consistent cocky voice, which is cheap for Claude to draft but expensive for one designer to keep in tune, and an all-blue low-poly glacier can read as samey, so variety must come from objects, sounds and vents rather than geometry.

## The judges

**Designer, 7.5/10.** The ember pot is the best constraint of the six and the least Dave-like thing in the batch: light and warmth as one object you handle, with three verbs (blow, feed, pocket), drain driven by terrain so route choice is the game, your body degrading in stages, no bar on screen, and the pocket turning the fail-state into a bet you make deliberately while your hands still work. The sakura as the Register's meter ('spring arrives exactly as fast as things go home') is the cleanest expression of the designer's Goal, and a glacier giving back a 1962 camera, a 1986 mail bag, claims from the dead and your own lost sled feels like a real place with history. What it lacks is life: nothing in the ice moves or wants anything, so half of Dave's generosity (creatures, small living wonder) is absent, an all-blue glacier can read as samey, and the dusk claims risk being lookups.

*Best idea in it:* A dimming pot of coals you read with your eyes and body instead of a bar, with the breast pocket as a pre-committed keep-one you choose while your hands still work.

*The one change:* Give the glacier a creature class: frozen things that wake as they thaw (a stiff crane, a 1962 expedition sled dog, ice-fish in meltwater pools, a bear fur that is still a bear) with behaviours that need the pick, warmth or a net, and that can be kept at Ume-ya; the out currently has objects and weather but nothing alive.

**Producer, 7.5/10.** The cleanest dial mapping of the six (coals = oxygen, coat = suit depth, sled = cargo, pick = harpoon) and the most physical constraint: one handled object with three verbs (blow, feed, pocket), terrain-driven drain that makes route choice the game, a pre-committed keep-one you tuck away while your hands still work, and a Hearth sink that squeezes against returns so over-collecting is never waste. Dusk claims give the home a manual burst at the opposite pace, and spring-as-scoreboard (bare to bloom as things go home) is the best 'what grows' line here. Costs the others avoid: a snow pass, four sakura states, hearth, bath and gate plus an 18-piece ice kit (about 8 Claude days) before the kit is a home, the toy drops the walkable kit scene for a grey plane, and an all-blue glacier is a stated samey risk with variety pushed onto writing a solo designer must keep in tune. Six-day toy, over the bar, but its question (does the glow alone, with no bar, make the player turn back and then go straight back in) is a genuine, failable test.

*Best idea in it:* The ember pot as a thing in your hands rather than a bar: its glow is sight and warmth at once, you can spend it (blow), feed it (burn a find) or save one thing from it (pocket), and the drain is written into the terrain, so every fork is a bet you can see.

*The one change:* Replace colour-graded ice tiers with lost places frozen into the glacier (a drowned hamlet, a lost August, a wartime camp), so each coat opens a place rather than a shade; that kills the samey risk, gives rings for two years, and lets the kit's own pieces appear frozen below.
