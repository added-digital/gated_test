Six independent concepts for a surface-and-underground loop game serving the Goal *the player feels accomplished*, written by six agents from six starting angles, then ranked by two judges (a designer lens, a producer lens). Scores are out of 10 per judge. The designer picks; the AI never does.

## The judges' verdicts

**Designer lens.** Put Yamabiko in front of the designer, with Thaw's ember pot already grafted onto it, and show Thaw second as the same game seen from the constraint. Be honest with them first: five of the six are one skeleton (lost things in a hole under the tea house, three dials, keep-one, a named return, a book) because the research's Dave skeleton is strong enough that every concept converged on it, so this is not six options but one game with five skins plus Radio Hollow; the real choices are what the hole holds and how the dial feels. Yamabiko answers the first best (lost places as rings, tsukumogami, a garden that comes up out of the well, a tengu who lies about carrying you up) and Thaw the second (a glowing pot you handle and read on your own body instead of a bar). Yamabiko is also the one where a visual person watches their own kit become the reward, and whose first ten minutes already contains a joke, a mystery and a bet. Its risks are the Dave-literalness of the dials and a seven-day toy that is really a slice; the ember graft fixes the first, and the toy should be cut to warmth, carry, one awake thing, Pim and Sudo. Mountain Keeps' Swallow must not be lost: it is the chapter-scale calm-break Yamabiko lacks. Radio Hollow is the only true alternative and belongs in the drawer as the 'what if' to return to; it is a better store-page idea than a better game for this designer.

**Producer lens.** I would put Yamabiko in front of the designer, with Thaw as the one alternative to read beside it. All six converge on the same skeleton (a tea house, a hole that keeps everything lost, Pim who remembers, three dials, keep-one), which is itself the finding: the conceit is settled, and the choice is about the home half, how the constraint feels in the hand, and the art bill. Yamabiko wins on the producer's axes: the kit is reused untouched, home growth is free because the garden is hidden kit pieces earned from below, rings-as-lost-places give variety inside one hole for two years with no new systems, and the loop is the most complete with a social progression that fixes Hwang's nameless-customer regret. Thaw wins on the constraint itself (the coal pot with blow, feed and pocket is the one dial design here that would be a game by itself) and its dusk claims are a better home burst than Yamabiko's wrap-label-bag Dawn Run; it loses on an eight-day art bill before the kit is a home and a glacier that risks reading as one blue corridor. The condition on either is the same: no toy in this set meets the 2-4 Claude day bar (5 to 7 days claimed, Yamabiko the worst at 7), so the first instruction is to cut the toy to Dave's own first-slice shape, one zone, one clock, one upgrade track, which for Yamabiko means keeping warmth, the bet, the Dawn Run and KEEP and dropping mon, the shop, awake creatures and the breath gate until the third unprompted descent has been seen. Dropwell's one-table rule and its sinking mechanic should be adopted whichever concept is chosen, because they are what keep 'add a lost thing forever' true at ten hours a week.

## Grafts the judges recommend onto the top concept

- (designer) From Thaw: replace Yamabiko's warmth bar and tea tiers with the ember pot. The hearth's coals in a clay pot are light and warmth at once; blow (spend heat for sight), feed (burn a wooden or cloth find for time, and Pim notices when it was somebody's), pocket (the one thing that comes home, chosen while your hands still work). Drain is driven by terrain (wind gaps double, the black lake triple, warm vents pause) so route choice is the game, and the body degrades in stages (breath, hunch, shaking hands, no grip) instead of a number.
- (designer) From Thaw: the two sakura are the Guestbook's meter (bare, bud, half, full) and the home starts in late winter, so the pass thaws exactly as fast as things go home; and the chapter-one climax puts the home's own warmth at stake (the hearth goes cold, snow blows onto the tatami, the fix is inside the Hollow).
- (designer) From What the Mountain Keeps: the Swallow as Yamabiko's chapter-scale calm-break. At scheduled story moments the mountain breathes IN rather than out: petals stream toward the well, the lid lifts, and something you placed in the garden slides down, so a later ring is entered to get your own garden back. Keep its forty-second scramble as the Help toy with a face, and its keep-one-by-touch text when you go numb ('something cold and round', 'something that is trying to lick you').
- (designer) From What the Mountain Keeps: the rope as a leash. Side-wells and longer ropes reach deeper rings but mean a longer walk back, so every reach upgrade raises the stakes on warmth; the dials pull against each other instead of each growing in isolation.
- (designer) From Dropwell: lost things sink as they are forgotten. What you saw in ring one last trip is in ring two now, what you dropped when numb is deeper and an awake thing may have taken it, so the bet never resets and old content retires itself; and the Lost Things table with a behaviour column (sits, drifts, flees, steals, follows, leads astray) as the single content unit, with the Sockrat as the first thief.
- (designer) From Dropwell and What Fell: the voice of the one who carries you up. Oba's dryness ('I'm saying it to the pond') and Tove counting the rescues ('Fourth time. I am not a stair.') fold into Pim's lie and the Yamabiko mystery, so the fail-state has a character keeping score.
- (designer) From What Fell: the tokonoma shelf as a content engine. An unclaimed thing is a person who has not arrived yet; a guest on the engawa browses the shelf and says 'that's my sister's', spawning a new character from an item already in the game. And leaving-days: some guests must go down the road by the third morning with or without their thing, so the surface has calendar pressure without a timer.
- (designer) From What Fell: the deepest rings hold lost nouns that are not objects (a lost tune, a lost Tuesday, a lost name, a lost temper), same pickup system, stranger things, so wonder deepens with the rope.
- (designer) From Radio Hollow: the Evening Show's one rule replaces the Dawn Run's wrap-label-bag. Right thing to the right person before the tea goes cold, with callers you have never met once Sudo carries strangers' things. And every return adds a layer to the home's ambient mix (a bell that rings noon again, a kettle-spirit's whistle, cicadas in the Lost Summer) so accomplishment is heard as well as placed; the lit windows of the village down the pass as a visible count of who has heard of the hanami place.
- (designer) From Radio Hollow: 'see or listen, pick one' in the Hollow. A clap lights the chamber's edges for a second but scares every awake thing on the ring, so the far-sound search and the tsukumogami hunt are in tension inside one verb.
- (designer) From Thaw: your own lost things come back. The furoshiki you left when you went numb turns up two rings down with your haul still inside it, moved and slightly changed, with a note from the umbrella.
- (producer) From Thaw: the pocket as pre-committed keep-one. You choose what survives the numb state in advance, at the well-head or in the cold while your hands still work; numbness takes everything else, so the bet is made by the player, not by a menu after the fade.
- (producer) From Dropwell: things sink as they are forgotten. Anything dropped or left in the Hollow moves one ring deeper per visit, so the fail-state's 'moved' becomes 'deeper', urgency exists without a timer, and every lost haul is a reason to buy rope and go back.
- (producer) From Dropwell: the one-table rule and the 30-minute budget. A lost thing is one prop, one row (ring, slots, pickup kind, material, behaviour, owner) and two lines, or it does not go in; anything needing new code arrives as a character request, never through the table.
- (producer) From Thaw: replace the Dawn Run's wrap-label-bag with description matching. Sudo reads a stranger's one-line description and the player matches it to a thing on the tokonoma before his tea goes cold, so the home burst uses memory and writing rather than packing.
- (producer) From What Fell: shelf recognition as the content engine. Guests on the engawa browse the tokonoma shelf and claim or recognise things ('that's my sister's'), spawning new characters and requests from props already built.
- (producer) From What the Mountain Keeps: the world-change rule. No return or KEEP placement lands without one visible or audible change at home (a lantern lights, the kettle changes note, a guest says a line that quotes you), so handover never reads as a click.
- (producer) From What the Mountain Keeps: keep-one by touch. When numb, the keep-one choice is offered by description rather than picture ('something cold and round'), which fits the fade and makes the bet a line of writing.
- (producer) From Radio Hollow: audible calm. Every kept thing and resident adds a layer to the tea house's ambient mix (the kettle-spirit's whistle, rain on the awake umbrella, Hatsu's pipe), so the home's calm is heard growing, not only seen.
- (producer) From Thaw: the kit's two sakura as the guestbook's meter, bare to bud to bloom across signatures, so 'the hanami place' at twenty is a tree the player watched fill in.
- (producer) From What Fell: Pim counts the carries. 'Fourth time. I am not a stair.' feeds directly into the Yamabiko mystery of who really carries you up.
